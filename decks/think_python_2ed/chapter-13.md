Q: What are the three main data structure decisions in the Markov analysis exercise?

Q: What are the five debugging strategies mentioned in the chapter?
A: Reading, Running, Ruminating, Rubberducking, and Retreating.

C: [Reading] involves examining your code and checking that it says what you meant to say.

C: [Running] experiments involves making changes and testing different versions of the program.

C: [Ruminating] means taking time to think about what kind of error it is and what information you can gather.

Q: What is rubber duck debugging?
A: Explaining the problem to an inanimate object (like a rubber duck), which often helps you find the answer before finishing the question.

C: [Retreating] involves backing off and undoing recent changes until you get back to a program that works and that you understand.

Q: What is "random walk programming"?
A: Making random changes until the program does the right thing, which can take a long time.

Q: Why might reading your code 100 times not help find an error?
A: If the problem is a conceptual misunderstanding, the error is in your head, not in the code.

Q: What should you do if there are too many errors or the code is too big and complicated?
A: Retreat by simplifying the program until you get to something that works and that you understand.

Q: Why are beginning programmers often reluctant to retreat?
A: Because they can't stand to delete a line of code, even if it's wrong.

Q: What is the recommended approach if you're reluctant to delete code?
A: Copy your program into another file before stripping it down, then copy pieces back one at a time.
